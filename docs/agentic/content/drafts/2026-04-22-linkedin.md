Sunday evening. The week's marking is done.

Now comes the other job: writing lesson plans for Monday.

A teacher in Ibadan told us she sets aside four hours every Sunday for this. Not because she's slow — she has been teaching JSS2 English for eleven years. But the templates she inherited were built around examples from British textbooks. Every week she has to swap out the references, find local analogies, check the curriculum sequence, and write objectives from scratch.

Four hours. Every Sunday. Before she can even think about what her students might struggle with on Monday morning.

We started building Awade for exactly this.

When a teacher on Awade opens a lesson plan, the curriculum structure is already there — aligned to the national curriculum for their country and grade level. The objectives are pre-filled. The content draws on local context, not borrowed examples.

What used to take four hours can now take forty minutes. The teacher still owns the plan — she edits, adjusts, adds what she knows about her class. But she is not starting from a blank page designed for someone else's classroom.

We have a long way to go. The platform is still early. But every week we hear from teachers who say they got their Sunday evenings back.

That is the thing we are building toward.

If you work with teachers in Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya, or anywhere across the continent — I would genuinely love to hear what the lesson-planning process looks like in your context. Drop a comment or send me a message.
